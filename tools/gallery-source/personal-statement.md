**I believe the artist is not the ultimate authority on their own art.**

…

That sounds absurd. After all, artists are the ones who conceive and create their work. Who could possibly understand what a piece of art is trying to say better than the person who made it?

But I believe that while artists can decide what they want to express, they cannot control what others ultimately see in their work. Once a work is shared, it takes on a life beyond its creator. People bring their own experiences, emotions, and perspectives to it, giving it meanings that the artist could never have created alone.

I trace this belief back to the Chinese exam-oriented education I grew up with. In literature classes, we were constantly asked, "What is the author trying to say?" Yet on exams, reading comprehension questions usually had only one or two accepted answers. Instead of being encouraged to interpret a text for ourselves, we were taught to follow a formula to arrive at the "correct" interpretation. Did the author actually mean what the answer key said? What if I understood the text differently? Nobody cared.

…

This summer, a Chinese animated film called *Niu Lai*, made on a budget of just 3,000 RMB, unexpectedly went viral for being spectacularly bad. Its terrible animation and storytelling became a social media sensation, drawing crowds to theaters. The internet exploded with discussions and fan creations. People analyzed its art style, debated its plot, rushed to cosplay its characters, and even drove up the price of a cryptocurrency inspired by the film.

I told a friend, "I don't like *Niu Lai*. It's not even a decent animated film. But it's a good piece of art."

People loved it, hated it, envied its success, turned it into a meme, a conversation starter, or a way to make money. Whatever the filmmakers originally intended, the audience had turned it into something much bigger. In a way, it was the audience that completed the work.

…

In one of my college photography classes, we regularly held critiques where everyone pinned their developed photographs to the wall. No captions. No explanations. Just the images.

For one assignment centered on reflections, I asked a music-loving friend to model for me. He held a CD in each hand, with the reflective sides facing him and parts of his face appearing on their surfaces. I framed the shot with his head partially blocking the view.

But when I presented the photograph, something unexpected happened. Because of the camera angle, lighting, and reflections, my professor and classmates couldn't agree on how many people were in the image or where they were positioned. Their disagreement soon turned into several entirely different stories about what was happening in the photograph.

…

Ironically, I had become the very author whose intentions I had spent years being asked to decipher in Chinese literature exams.

Of course I knew what I had photographed. But I couldn't control what others saw.

The audiences of *Niu Lai* and the people in my photography class had something in common: they found meanings that the creators had never anticipated. They weren't simply receiving a work of art. They were participating in the creation of its meaning.

As a filmmaker, I can choose how to frame a shot, where to make a cut, and what happens to my characters. But I cannot decide how an audience will understand the story.

And I don't want to.

…

But I want to take this idea one step further.

What if the audience could do more than interpret a story? What if they could help tell it through their own actions?

I call this exploration the "democratization of storytelling." My game, *Polaroid of Yesterday*, is one attempt to put that idea into practice.

The protagonist suffers from dissociative amnesia. Players use a Polaroid camera to photograph certain objects, triggering fragments of forgotten memories. What they choose to photograph, the order in which they discover those memories, and how they piece together the story all depend on their own actions.

At one point, players must reconstruct a sequence of events from the memories they've recovered. They have to decide which events actually happened and in what order. But I deliberately chose not to force them to find the single correct answer. After three attempts, they can move forward regardless of whether they got it right.

I'm less interested in whether players can guess the truth I had in mind than in how they arrive at their own understanding of the story, shaped by their experiences and judgments.

And then comes the very last scene.

The protagonist sits in front of a television. On the screen, the game begins again: the player learning to use the Polaroid camera, entering the first scene…

Beside the couch, another camera is recording everything.

I wanted this moment to make players reconsider the story they had just experienced, perhaps even question their own role in it.

The photos they took, the memories they uncovered, and the conclusions they reached weren't simply actions required to move the plot forward. Those actions were part of how the story came to be told.

The player was no longer just experiencing the narrative. They had become part of it.

…

Working on *Polaroid of Yesterday* left me with new questions. How can every game mechanic carry narrative meaning? How can I stay true to my creative vision while giving players more room to shape the story themselves?

What draws me to USC is not only its programs in Game Design and Interactive Media, but also the fact that both are rooted in the School of Cinematic Arts. I want to take full advantage of this interdisciplinary environment, continuing to explore cinema's visual language, narrative structures, and emotional expression while developing a deeper understanding of game mechanics and interaction design. I don't want to simply combine film and games. I want to explore how each can strengthen the other.

My goal isn't just to make games with more choices or more endings. Nor do I want to create mechanics simply because they're "fun," or visuals simply because they look "cool."

I want every design decision to be intentional. Whether it's a game mechanic, an interaction, or a visual choice, it should have a reason to exist. Above all, I want interaction itself to become an invitation to participate in the storytelling, an expressive language rather than merely a way to make the story more entertaining.

As a child, I was taught to search for the one correct interpretation hidden in every story. Now, as a creator, I want to do something different. Instead of telling a story on my own and asking players to understand it, I want to create experiences in which the story takes shape through our participation together.

I can decide what story I want to tell. But I don't want to decide what that story ultimately becomes for the people who experience it. I want players not only to find their own meaning in a story, but to become its co-storytellers.
